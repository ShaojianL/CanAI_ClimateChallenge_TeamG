# Community Crisis Reporting Map

## Presentation Script & Slide Design Guide

**Tagline:** *From scattered reports to shared awareness.*  
**Suggested presentation length:** 7–9 minutes, including the demo.

This presentation should tell a story rather than follow the structure of a conventional software demo: begin with a situation people in Newfoundland can relate to, reveal the information gap, show how the product addresses it, and bring the opening situation to life during the demo.

**The main theatrical moment:** A single resident reports an incident, the public map changes, and another resident receives a relevant alert on their phone.

---

# Slide 1 — What If Your City Could Talk?

**Purpose:** Grab the audience's attention before revealing the product.

### Speaking Notes

> Imagine this.
>
> It's 7:30 in the morning. A major snowstorm has just passed through St. John's.
>
> You're a nurse, and you have an early shift at the hospital.
>
> You get in your car, start driving, and halfway through your commute, you discover that a tree has fallen across the road.
>
> Now you have to turn around, find another route, and hope you make it to work on time.
>
> But here's the thing.
>
> Someone living on that street might have seen that tree fall an hour ago.
>
> **What if they could have told you before you even left home?**
>
> That's the idea behind our project: Community Crisis Reporting Map.

### Slide Design & Elements

- **Background:** A full-screen, darkened map of St. John's, showing roads and neighbourhoods.
- **Opening animation:** Start with just the map and a subtle snowstorm overlay.
- Place a small warning marker on one street, representing a fallen tree.
- Slowly zoom in on the warning marker as the opening story unfolds.
- **Title reveal:** After asking the central question, reveal the project name and tagline.

**Suggested text on slide:**

> WHAT IF YOUR CITY COULD TALK?
>
> Community Crisis Reporting Map
>
> *From scattered reports to shared awareness.*

**Theatrical element:** Begin with no conventional title slide visible. Let the map and the story introduce the problem. Reveal the project name only at the end.

# Slide 2 — The Problem: Information Exists. Awareness Doesn't.

**Purpose:** Explain the problem from both the government's and residents' perspectives.

### Speaking Notes

> The problem we are trying to solve is actually quite simple.
>
> Governments don't always have a complete, up-to-date picture of what's happening across their city.
>
> Traditional incident reporting can be slow. Someone notices an issue, they try to figure out who to contact, and that information has to make its way through the system.
>
> But when we're dealing with natural disasters, road obstructions, flooding, or other hazards, that delay can really matter.
>
> And on the other side, we have residents.
>
> Residents don't always know what's happening around them either.
>
> A road might be blocked, a neighbourhood might be flooded, or snow-clearing activity might be happening on their street.
>
> People often only find out when they encounter the problem themselves.
>
> **So we have two groups that need the same information, but no simple way for them to share it quickly.**
>
> And that's the gap we're trying to address.

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** Two-column split screen.

**Left — Governments**

- Illustration of a municipal operations dashboard with missing or delayed information.
- Scattered incident icons around a city.
- Minimal text:
  - Limited real-time visibility
  - Delayed reporting
  - Fragmented information

**Right — Residents**

- Illustration of a commuter facing a blocked road.
- A route on the map ends at an obstruction.
- Minimal text:
  - Unexpected hazards
  - Last-minute disruptions
  - Limited local awareness

**Centre:** A broken connection or dotted line between the two groups.

**Bottom statement:**

> The information is out there. It just isn't reaching the right people at the right time.

**Theatrical element:** Reveal the government's challenges first, then the residents' challenges, and finally connect both with the central problem statement.

# Slide 3 — The Solution: A City Powered by Its Community

**Purpose:** Introduce the product and explain the complete concept without going too deep into individual features.

### Speaking Notes

> So how do we solve this?
>
> Our solution is a community-powered crisis reporting platform.
>
> The idea is that anyone who notices an issue in their neighbourhood can report it.
>
> It could be a fallen tree, a traffic accident, a flooded road, or even snow-clearing activity.
>
> Once a report is submitted, AI processes the information, identifies potentially related reports, and creates a clear alert.
>
> That alert appears on a publicly accessible map, where other residents can see what's been reported around them.
>
> Authorized government users can also have a broader view of flagged issues, helping them understand where attention might be needed.
>
> But we wanted to go one step further.
>
> **People shouldn't have to constantly check a map to find out whether something has happened on their street.**
>
> They should be able to subscribe to the areas they care about and receive relevant alerts automatically.
>
> So instead of waiting for information to reach you, we want to make it possible for your community to keep you informed.

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** A central visual of the product surrounded by four connected steps.

1. **Report:** A resident submits an incident.
2. **Process:** AI checks, categorizes, and consolidates reports.
3. **Visualize:** The public map displays the informational alert.
4. **Notify:** Relevant subscribers receive an update.

**Central graphic:** Map of St. John's with several illustrative incident markers.

**Suggested slide text:**

> ONE PLATFORM. TWO PERSPECTIVES.
>
> **Residents:** Report, explore, subscribe.
>
> **Governments:** See, understand, follow up.

**Animation:** A report icon appears, moves through an AI symbol, becomes a map marker, and then sends out notification waves.

**Important:** Keep the government view described as a planned capability rather than an integration with an existing municipal emergency system.

# Slide 4 — Feature 1: See Something? Report It.

### Speaking Notes

>
> Instead of assuming someone else has already reported it, you can open our platform and submit a report.

>
> The system now has information that other people in the community might find useful.
>

>
> The goal is simple.
>
> **If someone sees a problem, they should have an easy way to let their community know.**

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** Large mobile phone mockup on the left, short feature highlights on the right.

**Mobile interface:**

- Street / location selector
- Incident category
- Description field
- Submit Report button

**Example report:**

> **Fallen Tree**
>
> Location: Selected pilot street
>
> "Large tree has fallen across the road. Vehicles appear unable to pass."

**Right-side text:**

> NOTICE IT.
>
> REPORT IT.
>
> HELP YOUR COMMUNITY.

# Slide 5 — Feature 2: From Reports to Real-Time Awareness

**Purpose:** Explain how AI adds value and how the resulting alerts appear on the map.

### Speaking Notes

> Now, getting reports is only half the challenge.
>
> Imagine that five different residents report the same fallen tree.
>
> We don't want five separate alerts cluttering the map or five notifications going out about what might be the same incident.
>
> This is where AI comes in.
>
> Our AI processes incoming reports, checks for potential duplicates, identifies related incidents, and organizes the information into something easier to understand.
>
> It also assigns an informational severity level: Low, Medium, or High.
>
> And instead of showing a collection of individual messages, the platform can present a consolidated alert describing what has been reported, where it happened, and when the information was received.
>
> These alerts appear directly on our interactive map.
>
> Residents can explore different streets and read the details, while authorized municipal users could use a broader view to identify areas that may require attention.
>
> Now, one thing that's important here: **AI processing doesn't mean an incident has been independently verified.**
>
> Our platform makes that distinction clear, so people know they're looking at community-reported information rather than an official safety advisory.
>
> The goal isn't to replace existing emergency services. It's to make community information more accessible and useful.

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** Two-part composition.

**Left — AI Processing**

Three small report cards:

- "Tree blocking street"
- "Large branch across roadway"
- "Road blocked after storm"

Arrows merge into an AI processing symbol, producing one consolidated alert.

**Right — Interactive Map**

A map showing:

- Street geometry
- Severity-coded incident markers
- A selected alert with details
- Timestamp and reported/unverified status

**Example alert:**

> **Reported Road Obstruction**
>
> Informational Severity: High
>
> A fallen tree has reportedly blocked a section of the road.
>
> Status: Community-reported · Unverified
>
> Last updated: 7:45 AM

**Suggested bottom statement:**

> Multiple reports. One clearer picture.

**Theatrical element:** Animate the three reports merging into one map alert. The map then zooms toward the incident location.

**Design note:** Severity should be visually distinguishable through icons, labels, and accessible colours—not colour alone. The displayed severity is an illustrative classification, not an official safety rating.

# Slide 6 — Feature 3: Your Streets. Your Alerts.

**Purpose:** Present the personalized notification system as the major feature that differentiates the platform from a standard reporting map.

### Speaking Notes

> Now imagine going back to that nurse from the beginning of our presentation.
>
> They don't have time to open a map every morning to check for road closures or fallen trees.
>
> And they probably don't care about every incident happening across St. John's.
>
> They care about the streets they use.
>
> So we introduced subscriptions.
>
> Users can subscribe to specific streets, one of our pilot districts, or all three districts.
>
> They can also decide what kind of alerts they want to receive.
>
> Maybe they only want High-severity reports. Or maybe they want to know about any snow-clearing updates happening in their area.
>
> Once the AI has processed a relevant report, the system can notify matching subscribers.
>
> Our preferred delivery method is SMS, with email as a fallback if free text-message delivery isn't feasible.
>
> **It's not about receiving more notifications. It's about receiving the right information, about the right place, when it matters.**
>
> That means our nurse could learn about the reported road obstruction before starting their commute.

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** A large mobile notification mockup taking up most of the slide.

**Left — Subscription Preferences**

A simple interface showing:

- Area: Selected pilot street
- Severity: Medium + High
- Categories: Road obstruction, flooding
- Notifications: Enabled

**Right — Notification**

> **Community Alert**
>
> A fallen tree has been reported on a street you follow.
>
> Severity: High
>
> Reported at 7:45 AM
>
> Community-reported · Unverified
>
> View on map

**Suggested heading:**

> YOU CHOOSE WHAT MATTERS.

**Theatrical element:** At the moment you say "the system can notify matching subscribers," play one subtle notification sound while the phone notification appears.

**Important:** The MVP subscription scope is a street, one of St. John's Center, St. John's East, or St. John's West, or all three pilot districts—not the entire city.

# Slide 7 — Live Demo: One Report. One Map. One Alert.

**Purpose:** Bring the opening story full circle and demonstrate the complete product journey.

### Speaking Notes

> Okay, so we've talked about the problem, the solution, and the features.
>
> But what does this actually look like?
>
> Let's go back to our original scenario.
>
> A snowstorm has just passed through St. John's. A tree has fallen across a street, and someone nearby notices it.
>
> **Let's see what happens when they report it.**

**[Begin live demonstration]**

> First, we have our public map. Users can explore streets and see any available community-reported alerts.
>
> Now, let's submit an example incident.
>
> We'll select a street within our pilot area, choose 'Fallen Tree,' and provide a short description.
>
> And we'll submit that report.
>
> Behind the scenes, our AI processes the information, identifies the incident category, checks for related reports, and prepares an alert with an informational severity level.
>
> Now let's return to the map.
>
> And here we can see the newly processed report, displayed on the corresponding street.
>
> If we select the alert, we can read the reported conditions, severity, timestamp, and verification status.
>
> But remember, we have another resident who has already subscribed to this street.
>
> So now, the final part of our demonstration.

**[Trigger the notification workflow]**

> If everything matches their subscription preferences, they receive an alert about the incident.
>
> They didn't need to be the person who reported it.
>
> And they didn't need to open the map to look for it.
>
> Someone else in their community saw something, and now they have information that could help them plan their journey.
>
> **One person reports. An entire subscribed community can benefit.**

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** Minimal. The application itself should be the focus.

**Suggested opening slide text:**

> LET'S PUT IT TO THE TEST.
>
> A fallen tree. One resident. One report.

**Demo sequence:**

1. Start with the public map showing the selected pilot area.
2. Show a resident's active street subscription and severity filters.
3. Submit a clearly labelled synthetic fallen-tree report.
4. Show the AI processing outcome.
5. Return to the map and reveal the newly generated alert.
6. Show the alert information and unverified status.
7. Trigger the subscription notification.
8. Display the received SMS or email, if real delivery is working.

### Theatrical Elements

**The phone reveal:** Have a teammate stand near the presenter with a phone subscribed to the demonstration street. If real message delivery has been tested, let the notification arrive on their phone. The teammate can briefly hold it up or mirror it onto the presentation screen.

**Bring back the story:** End the demo by returning to the nurse from Slide 1, connecting the technology to the human problem.

**Demo safeguards:**

- Use only synthetic incident reports and authorized test destinations.
- Do not claim an SMS was sent if only a simulated delivery is available.
- If AI processing or real delivery is unavailable, label the demonstrated step as simulated or pre-recorded.
- Keep a prepared demo recording as a backup, without presenting it as live.

# Slide 8 — Next Steps: From Prototype to Practical Use

**Purpose:** Show that the team understands what remains to be built, tested, and validated.

### Speaking Notes

> So what comes next?
>
> Our hackathon prototype is really just the beginning.
>
> The first step is making sure the core reporting process works reliably, from submitting an incident to displaying it on the map and notifying the right subscribers.
>
> We also want to improve how the AI handles uncertain or conflicting reports, because the last thing we want is to spread misinformation during an actual emergency.
>
> Another priority is testing the notifications and making sure people receive only relevant updates.
>
> And finally, we want to explore how this information could be useful for governments and municipal operations.
>
> The long-term goal isn't just a platform that displays pins on a map.
>
> **It's a platform that helps communities stay informed and gives decision-makers a clearer picture of what's being reported around them.**

### Slide Design & Elements

**Layout:** Three-stage horizontal roadmap.

**Stage 1 — Build & Validate**

- Complete the reporting-to-alert workflow
- Verify map and street coverage
- Test real notification delivery

**Stage 2 — Improve Trust**

- Refine AI severity and consolidation
- Handle uncertain and conflicting reports
- Improve alert corrections and withdrawals

**Stage 3 — Explore Partnerships**

- Test usability with residents
- Explore municipal use cases
- Evaluate expanded coverage

**Suggested heading:**

> BUILD THE FOUNDATION. EARN TRUST. EXPAND RESPONSIBLY.

**Visual:** A route progressively extending across a city map, with three milestone markers.

# Presentation-Wide Creative Direction

## Visual Identity

**Theme:** A modern city operations interface — somewhere between a navigation app, an incident map, and a clean public-sector dashboard.

- **Backgrounds:** Dark navy or charcoal, with a few bright accents.
- **Map visuals:** Real street geometry where available, simplified to avoid overwhelming the audience.
- **Typography:** Large headlines, short statements, minimal bullet points.
- **Iconography:** Location pins, warning symbols, AI processing icons, notification bells, and street-selection controls.
- **Animations:** Prefer purposeful motion that illustrates the movement of information through the system.

## Storytelling Structure

| Act | Slides | Audience experience |
|---|---|---|
| **Act 1: The Problem** | 1–2 | "I've experienced something like that." |
| **Act 2: The Idea** | 3–6 | "I understand how this could help." |
| **Act 3: The Proof** | 7 | "That actually connects the reporting and notification journey." |
| **Act 4: The Vision** | 8–9 | "I can see how this could grow." |

## Three Moments Worth Rehearsing

**1. The opening:** Deliver the snowstorm scenario slowly, then pause briefly before asking, "What if they could have told you before you even left home?"

**2. The demo reveal:** Make the map update and notification the centre of attention. If the delivery channel works live, coordinate the phone notification with the speaker's narration.

**3. The closing:** Return to the nurse and the fallen tree. The audience should leave remembering the human problem, not just the software.

## Final Presenter Notes

The product is a hackathon prototype for community situational awareness. Describe AI-generated severities as informational, not verified safety assessments. Do not suggest that public agencies have formally adopted the platform, that incidents are independently confirmed, or that notification providers have been integrated unless those capabilities have actually been demonstrated.

Keep the presentation centered on one promise: **A community observation should be easier to turn into useful, local information for the people who need it.**

## Most Important Presentation Decision

Make **Slide 7 the centerpiece of the pitch**. Slides 1–6 establish the story and the product; the demo delivers the payoff. A phone notification, particularly on a real device, will be more memorable than another feature screenshot.
